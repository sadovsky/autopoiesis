A sandbox report

Genes on the Wire

A population of programs, an environment that keeps changing the question, and executable genes that move between machines over TCP. What spreads sideways, what has to be found, and where the finding stops.

Subject the hgt crate Runs 8–16 seeds per condition Determinism one seed, one run Tests 60

Horizontal gene transfer is the reason a resistance gene can appear in a bacterium whose ancestors never had it. This is a small sandbox that puts the same mechanism into software: nodes hold genes that are actual bytecode, an environment poses a stressor that a gene has to compute the answer to, and genes move between nodes by conjugation, by uptake of the DNA of the dead, and by phages — over a deterministic in-process transport, or over real sockets between operating-system processes.

Four results. Transfer keeps populations alive across environmental shifts that kill every population without it, and it does so before the shift rather than after. Genes can also be found — but only inside a horizon whose two halves are a slope and a cliff. Sharing half-finished genes widens the search and loses what it finds; splicing them keeps what is found without widening the search. And a free rider that takes genes and offers none takes over the population, which survives anyway.

1 The question

Evolution in software is usually modelled vertically: a program is copied, mutated, scored, and the good copies have more children. That is a tree. Biology's more disruptive trick is the graph laid over that tree — a gene crossing from one lineage to another, sometimes across species, carried by a plasmid or a virus. Antibiotic resistance spreads that way faster than any lineage can evolve it.

The question this sandbox is built to make measurable is not does transfer happen, which you can arrange by fiat, but what transfer is worth: what it buys a population, when it buys it, and what it costs. Everything below is measured from a run, not asserted about a model.

2 The model

Genes are programs

A gene is up to 64 bytes of code for a small accumulator machine: low nibble opcode, high nibble operand, fifteen instructions, one byte each. One byte per instruction is the load-bearing choice — every byte string is a valid program, so a mutation is a small edit rather than a frame shift, and a truncated transfer is a shorter gene rather than garbage.

A gene's identity is the hash of its code. "The same gene" is therefore a fact about the bytes: allele frequency needs no registry, and a mutated copy is honestly a different allele rather than a damaged one.

The stressor, and what answers it

Each tick the environment poses a payload word. The correct response is rotate_left(payload ^ key, rot), with the key and rotation derived from the run's seed. A node survives if one of its genes computes that. The schedule is a pure function of (seed, tick), so every node derives it independently and there is no environment server sitting at the centre of a supposedly decentralised network.

Resistance is a program, and the sandbox will disassemble one for you:

Fourteen of those twenty bytes are the key, built a nibble at a time. That detail turns out to set the shape of everything in section 5.

Living, dividing, dying

A node pays to stay alive and pays again per gene it carries, then runs its genes against the stressor until one answers, paying for each trial. Above an energy threshold it divides, and the child inherits the genome with point mutations. At zero energy it dies. At the population ceiling a birth displaces somebody, chosen at random.

An exact answer earns full credit; a near miss earns credit in proportion to how close it got. That single knob decides whether working genes can only ever be inherited or received, or can be found.

How genes move

  • Conjugation — a three-message handshake: offer, request, transfer. A donor cannot push code into a node that did not ask for it.
  • Transformation — a dying node broadcasts its mobile genes; peers hold them as fragments and take them up at a rate. Free DNA comes from the dead.
  • Transduction — a phage needs no handshake and no consent. It infects, sometimes kills, repackages whatever the new host holds, and hops on.

Arrivals are graded by a restriction barrier — strain distance sets the odds of refusal, so transfer is a rate rather than a certainty. On top of it, an optional acquired immune memory lets a node that survived a phage cut that gene on sight. What a node offers and accepts is a heritable trait, not a global setting.

3 Method

A run in the simulated transport is fully described by (config, seed): one RNG stream drawn from in a fixed order, deterministic tie-breaks, and a world hash that two runs at one seed must agree on. Over TCP each process holds a deme and routes envelopes by node id — ownership is arithmetic, so no registry or coordinator is needed to move a gene across a machine boundary. That path is not deterministic and is not meant to be; it exists to show the transfer is network traffic and not a metaphor.

All measurement is derived from an event stream — births, acquisitions, losses, deaths, transfer attempts, per-tick aggregates — and nothing else. The analyzer reconstructs each genome from those records rather than reading the world, so measuring live and re-deriving from a log later are the same computation, verified byte-identical rather than asserted. Nothing in the log is a statistic: energy goes in as an integer total and the mean is computed downstream, because a float does not survive a JSON round trip bit-for-bit.

The metric that matters most is incongruence: the share of a gene's carriers that received it sideways rather than inheriting it. It is identically zero when no mechanism is switched on, which makes it a test as well as a measurement — and it is the thing that makes a gene tree disagree with a family tree, which is the signature that told biologists lateral transfer was happening at all.

The column that explains the rest is the fourth. It is not a measure of rescue; it is how common the answering gene already was at the moment the stressor arrived. With transfer on it is a half to a third of the population; without it, nothing. Transfer does not rescue a population after the crisis. It distributes the gene before there is one.

Transformation alone never manages it, for a reason the model did not have to be told: free DNA comes from the dead, and the dead are mostly those who lacked the gene worth having.

5 Can a gene be found?

Everything above moves genes that already exist. The second half of the sandbox asks whether a lineage can arrive at one: a single stressor that never shifts, an income a population can survive on while it searches, and founders a known number of bit flips from a gene that works. Transfer helps a search — but only once it is allowed to move things that do not work yet. A node offers a gene when it has seen it work, which makes a half-finished answer nobody's to give; lifting that rule finds the gene in every seed at four bit flips and in half again as many at twelve. What it does not do is keep it. Counting nodes still able to answer at the end of a run: six to eight when only proven genes move, zero to two when everything moves.

Sharing everything explores; sharing what works consolidates. They are different jobs, and in this model they trade off against each other.

What the horizon is made of

The wall at sixteen bits is not a mystery, and it is worth stating exactly because it is arithmetic rather than observation. A gene whose key is d bits wrong emits an answer d bits wrong, so it scores (16 − d) / 16 above chance. At sixteen it is at chance, and there is no slope left to climb. The other half of the landscape is the rotation, and it is flat: every wrong rotation moves every bit of the answer, so no key is worth more than any other until the rotation is right.

One bit of rotation error is a single lucky flip away, and a population crosses it in two hundred ticks. Two bits is a valley: the intermediate state pays nothing, so nothing selects for crossing it, and in sixteen seeds and ten thousand ticks nobody does. The population is not searching a hard problem badly. It is searching a smooth problem with a cliff nested inside it, which is a fair description of most real fitness landscapes.

Splicing

Transfer moves genes whole. Pairing it with recombination — splice the arrival into a resident copy at a crossover point, so what is integrated is a program neither node had — is the only way two lineages' partial answers can combine.

Splicing is not a better search: it finds the gene in slightly fewer seeds, because it breaks as many half-answers as it completes, and it does nothing at all beyond the horizon, where no hybrid can be better at anything. What it changes is what happens next. Where an answer is found, five to fifteen times as many nodes still hold one when the run ends. Recombination here is not a way of finding things. It is a ratchet.

6 Three shorter results

Free riders take over, and it does not matter

Eight of forty-eight founders start selfish — they accept genes and offer none — and the policy is inherited. Their share rises from a sixth to two thirds in three thousand ticks, and to four fifths when policy is allowed to mutate. All eight seeds survive anyway: a phage and a corpse need no donor's consent, so a population whose donors have been out-competed still moves genes. A world with only conjugation would not be so lucky.

Kills fall by a factor of six; a third of the population's transduced gene flow goes with them. An immune memory cannot tell a parasite from a gene you are going to need.

Cutting the network kills the population

With one founder holding each future gene and the network split from the start, two of eight seeds survive, against eight of eight when it is whole — and healing the split at tick 400 does not help, because the first shift is at 300 and by then it has happened. The measurement that makes it legible is per-side: the worst-off side of a cut network reaches zero nodes able to answer, where a whole one never falls below twenty-two.

7 Four things that were wrong first

Each of these was in the model before a measurement removed it. They are the useful part of a report like this, so they are listed rather than quietly fixed.

  • Unbounded genomes. Every mutated copy in flight is a new gene, so a population accumulates every copy that ever passed through it and starves under the upkeep. Adding more transfer mechanisms made survival worse until genomes were capped.
  • Deleting long-disused genes. The obvious fix for the above, and exactly backwards: it removes the spare genes — the reservoir the population needs at the next shift — while leaving the junk that arrived most recently.
  • Evicting the weakest node at the population ceiling. Sounds like selection; is the opposite. The weakest node is usually one that has just divided and handed half its energy to a child, so it selects against reproducing. Random eviction — a Moran step — is both simpler and less wrong.
  • Mutation as byte replacement. Replacing a byte changes the opcode fifteen times in sixteen, so nearly every mutation is fatal and no lineage can walk anywhere. A single bit flip lands in the operand half the time and leaves the instruction doing the same thing to a slightly different value. Nothing could be discovered until this changed.

A fifth, smaller one: the fitness signal was noise for a while, because a gene with the wrong rotation scores differently on every payload. Posing the stressor with several payloads per tick and averaging was what let selection hold on to a real gain.

8 What this model is not

The stressor is a single hidden function, so "fitness" is one dimension and there is no ecology: nodes do not eat each other, cooperate on anything, or occupy space. Recombination is one crossover point, with no homology search and no indels, so every gene is exactly as long as the one it came from. The interpreter is safe because it is tiny, not because it is hardened — this is not a claim about sandboxing untrusted code. The socket path is deliberately not deterministic and has no global observer: each process measures its own deme, because in a real distributed system that is all anyone has.

And the headline numbers are eight to sixteen seeds with medians, not confidence intervals. Differences of one or two seeds in the discovery tables are inside the noise, and are described above as trends rather than results.
