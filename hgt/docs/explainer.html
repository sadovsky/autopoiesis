Small experiments

Programs That Swap Genes

We built a tiny world where software passes working code to strangers, the way bacteria do. Here is what it taught us.

Bacteria have a trick that looks like cheating. When one of them works out how to survive an antibiotic, it doesn't have to pass that ability to its children and wait. It can hand the gene sideways — to a neighbour, to a stranger, sometimes to a completely different species. That's why resistance spreads through a hospital faster than any single bacterial family tree could carry it.

Biologists call it horizontal gene transfer. Everything in your biology textbook about traits passing from parent to child is the vertical kind. This is the other one.

We wondered what happens if you give software the same trick — not as a metaphor, but literally: little programs, holding little programs, mailing them to each other over a network. So we built it.

What's in the box

1

Little programs that live and die. Each one has an energy budget. It pays rent every tick. If it runs out, it dies. If it does well, it splits in two and its child inherits a slightly mutated copy of everything it had.

2

A world that keeps changing the question. Every tick the environment shouts a number, and a program has to shout back the right answer to get paid. Every three hundred ticks the rule for computing that answer changes completely.

3

Genes that are actually code. A gene is twenty bytes of bytecode that computes the answer. Not a flag saying "resistant" — an actual little program, which runs, and which a single wrong byte will break.

4

Three ways to pass one on. Hand it to a neighbour who asks for it. Leak it into the neighbourhood when you die, for whoever picks it up. Or let a virus carry it off to somebody who never asked.

At the start, almost nobody can answer the questions that haven't been asked yet. Two programs out of forty-eight hold the gene for the next crisis. Two hold the one after that. Everyone else is living happily on the answer to today.

Then the question changes

Turn the sharing off, and the story is short. The first time the world changes, the population is fine: enough of the founders' descendants happen to carry the new answer. The second time, it isn't. Every single run died, at almost exactly the same moment.

That last number is the one that surprised us, and it changed how we thought about the whole thing.

Sharing isn't a rescue. It's insurance.

We assumed transfer would look like a rescue: crisis hits, everyone starts dying, the gene races through the survivors, population recovers. That's not what happens.

By the time the new question arrives, half the population already has the answer. The gene had been quietly circulating for hundreds of ticks — copied between strangers who had no use for it, because it cost almost nothing to carry and somebody offered. When the crisis lands, most of the population simply isn't affected.

Without sharing, the same gene sits in the two programs that were born with it, and those two have to be lucky enough to still be alive. Usually they aren't.

Why the "dead people's DNA" route is the weak one

Of the three ways to pass a gene on, the one where you leak your genes as you die works worst — and once you see why, it's obvious. The programs that die are mostly the ones that didn't have the useful gene. Inheriting from the dead means inheriting from the people the crisis already selected against.

Can a program work out an answer on its own?

Everything above just moves genes that already exist somewhere. So we asked a harder question: can a lineage invent one? We started populations a known distance from a working gene — four wrong bits, eight, twelve, sixteen — and let them mutate.

Four bits away, they walk to it. Sixteen bits away, nobody ever gets there, in any run we did. And the reason turns out to be arithmetic rather than bad luck: each wrong bit costs exactly a sixteenth of the credit a program earns, so at sixteen wrong bits it earns nothing at all. There's no longer any signal saying "warmer". Evolution can't climb a hill that isn't there.

Then we found something better. The gene has two parts: a long number, and a small "rotate by this much" setting. The long number is a hill — get one bit closer, earn a bit more. The rotate setting is a cliff. Every wrong value scrambles the answer completely, so there's no partial credit for being nearly right.

One step across the gap is a coin flip you'll eventually win. Two steps is a valley, and nothing will pay you to cross it. Real evolution has this problem constantly; it was oddly satisfying to watch a toy have it too.

Three more things we didn't expect

  • Freeloaders win, and it doesn't matter. We gave a few programs a rule: take genes, never give any. Their descendants went from an eighth of the population to two thirds. The population survived anyway — because viruses and dead bodies don't ask permission, so genes kept moving even after the generous programs had been out-competed.
  • An immune system costs you the thing it protects. We let programs remember viruses that hurt them and refuse those genes forever. Virus deaths dropped six-fold. So did a third of all the useful genes they received — because a memory can't tell a parasite from something you'll need next week.
  • Cutting the network in half kills it. Split the population into two halves that can't talk, and six runs in eight die. The gene that answers the next question is on one side of the wall; the question is asked on both.

The bit that's not a metaphor

All of that runs inside one process, which is how you get repeatable numbers. But the same thing runs as four separate operating-system processes, talking over real TCP connections on real ports, with each message a line of JSON you could read with nc.

In that version, only the first process starts with the genes for the later crises. The other three start with nothing spare. They survive — on code that arrived as bytes on a socket from a process they never descended from. Turn the sharing off and every one of them dies.

Where this came from: a small Rust sandbox called hgt, built alongside a separate simulation about self-maintaining systems. Every number here comes from a run you can repeat: one seed produces one world, exactly, every time.
